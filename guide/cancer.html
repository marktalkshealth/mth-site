Chapter 2

Not all luck, not all genes

In 2022 there were about 18.7 million new cancers worldwide. Researchers estimate about 7.1 million of them were linked to risk factors that can be changed.

That's roughly 4 in 10. Smoking, alcohol, infections, excess weight, inactivity and diet are a big part of that picture.

At the same time, cancer is showing up in younger people, especially colorectal cancer in people under 50. Researchers are still working out exactly why, but the modern diet, rising weight and sitting all day are among the leading suspects.

I want to be very clear about something. No habit guarantees anything. Plenty of people who did everything right still get cancer, and it is never their fault. But risk is a numbers game, and the research shows food, movement and weight shift those numbers in study after study.

Chapter 3

What the research shows

The big picture

Nature Medicine 2026
About 7.1 million of 18.7 million new cancers worldwide in 2022 were linked to risk factors we can change.

A global analysis of preventable cancer.

Food and drink

BMJ 2018
Every extra 10% of ultra-processed food in the diet was linked to 12% higher cancer risk in 104,980 adults.

An observational link from the French NutriNet-Santé study.

BMJ 2019
Every extra 100 ml of sugary drinks a day, about a third of a can, was linked to 18% higher cancer risk.

Even 100% fruit juice was included in this link.

Gut 2021
2 or more sugary drinks a day were linked to about double the risk of colorectal cancer before age 50 in women.

One of the first studies looking at what might be behind the rise in early colon cancer.

Lancet 2019
High-fibre eaters had 15% to 30% lower risk of colorectal cancer and early death across 185 studies and 58 trials.

A major review commissioned by the World Health Organization.

JAMA Internal Medicine 2015
Women on a Mediterranean diet with extra virgin olive oil had 68% lower breast cancer risk.

From the PREDIMED trial. The number of cases was small, so treat this as promising rather than proven.

Movement

JAMA Internal Medicine 2016
In 1.44 million adults, higher activity was linked to lower risk of 13 different cancers.

Including colon, breast, liver, kidney and several others.

NEJM 2025 · CHALLENGE trial
A 3-year exercise program after colon cancer treatment cut cancer coming back or death by 28%.

A randomised trial, the strongest kind of evidence. Exercise worked like a treatment.

JAMA Oncology 2023
About 4.5 minutes a day of short huffing-and-puffing bursts was linked to 20% fewer cancers in non-exercisers.

Think fast stair climbing, carrying heavy shopping or walking hard uphill, in short bursts during daily life.

Timing and weight

JAMA Oncology 2016
Fasting under 13 hours overnight was linked to 36% higher breast cancer recurrence risk.

Compared with women who went 13 hours or more without food overnight.

JAMA 2022
Major weight loss after surgery was linked to 32% lower obesity-related cancer risk and 48% lower cancer death.

This shows that losing excess weight can lower risk, not just that being heavier raises it.

No single habit guarantees anything. But food, movement and weight shift cancer risk in study after study.
Chapter 4

The 5 levers that matter most

01

Move every day, and add short bursts

This is the strongest lever in this guide. More activity was linked to lower risk of 13 cancers, and after colon cancer an exercise program cut recurrence or death by 28% in a randomised trial. If you don't exercise, even 4.5 minutes a day of short breathless bursts was linked to fewer cancers.

This week: Walk every day. Add 3 or 4 one-minute bursts where you're breathing hard, like taking the stairs fast.
02

Cut the sugary drinks

Sugary drinks show up in both overall cancer risk and early colon cancer. Even a third of a can a day was linked to higher risk. This one change is easy to measure and easy to stick with.

This week: Replace every sugary drink and juice with water, sparkling water, tea or coffee.
03

Eat more fibre and less processed food

High-fibre eaters had 15% to 30% lower colorectal cancer risk across a huge review. Ultra-processed food went the other way. Beans, lentils, vegetables, fruit, whole grains, nuts and seeds are where the fibre lives.

This week: Add one serving of beans or lentils a day. Swap one packaged snack for fruit or nuts.
04

Give your body a 13-hour overnight break

In women with early breast cancer, fasting less than 13 hours overnight was linked to higher recurrence. A 13-hour break is easy for most people. Finish dinner by 7 and have breakfast at 8.

This week: Stop eating 13 hours before breakfast. No late-night snacks.
05

Work toward a healthy weight

Excess weight is linked to at least 13 cancers. The surgery study is important because it shows losing weight lowers risk too. You don't need surgery to get there. The other 4 levers in this guide all push weight in the right direction.

This week: Cook with olive oil and fill half your plate with vegetables. Let the weight follow the habits.

Chapter 6

Mistakes that keep people stuck

  • Thinking it's all genetics so nothing matters. About 4 in 10 cancers are linked to things we can change.
  • Thinking healthy habits mean you can skip screenings. They work together. Habits lower risk, screenings catch it early.
  • Trusting miracle cures online. If someone says a supplement or diet cures cancer, walk away.
  • Thinking juice is healthy. In the BMJ study, fruit juice counted as a sugary drink.
  • Waiting for the perfect workout plan. Walking and short bursts count.
  • Feeling guilty. Cancer is never anyone's fault. This is about adding good things, not blame.
Chapter 7

Safety first

Please read this. If you are in cancer treatment, never stop or delay it. Talk to your oncologist before fasting, starting a new exercise program or making big diet changes, since some treatments change what's safe for you. Keep up with your recommended screenings. See your doctor about any new lump, unexplained weight loss, blood in your stool or urine, a mole that changes, or a cough that won't go away. This guide is education, not medical advice.
