Chapter 2

The story you were sold

For most of the last 60 years, the message was simple. Fat is the enemy. Cut the fat and save your heart.

So the food industry did. Shelves filled with low-fat everything. And to make low-fat food taste good, they added sugar. Lots of it.

Then in 2016, researchers dug up internal documents showing the sugar industry had funded Harvard research in the 1960s that played down sugar's role in heart disease and shifted the blame onto fat. That research shaped decades of advice.

Fat isn't off the hook entirely. Artificial trans fats turned out to be a real problem, and so does a diet heavy in fried, processed food. But the newer trials tell a bigger story. Sugar, heavy processing, sitting, salt, stress and even loneliness all play a part. And everyday habits make a measurable difference.

Chapter 3

What the research shows

The food industry's role

JAMA Internal Medicine 2016
Internal documents show the sugar industry funded 1960s Harvard research that played down sugar's role and blamed fat.

This shaped public health advice for a generation.

JAMA Internal Medicine 2014
Getting 25% or more of calories from added sugar was linked to nearly 3 times the risk of dying from heart disease.

Compared with people getting less than 10% of their calories from added sugar.

JAMA Cardiology 2017
After New York restricted trans fats, hospital admissions for heart attack and stroke fell 6.2%.

A real-world experiment. Change the food supply and hearts respond.

Heart 2021
The highest fried-food eaters had 28% more major heart events and 37% more heart failure.

A review of studies covering hundreds of thousands of people.

BMJ 2019
Every 10% more ultra-processed food in the diet was linked to 12% higher heart disease risk.

From a large French study following more than 100,000 adults.

What protects your heart

NEJM 2018 · PREDIMED
Adding olive oil or nuts to a Mediterranean diet cut heart attacks, strokes and heart deaths by about 30% in 7,447 people.

One of the most important nutrition trials ever run. Notice that the groups eating more fat from olive oil and nuts did best.

NEJM 1997 · DASH
Changing what was on the plate lowered blood pressure by about 11 points in people with hypertension within 8 weeks.

More vegetables, fruit, beans and low-fat dairy. Less processed food. That's a drop on par with some medications.

NEJM 2021 · SSaSS
In 21,000 people, a salt substitute cut strokes by 14% and deaths by 12%.

Swapping regular salt for a potassium-based substitute.

Movement, rest and people

JAMA 2020
8,000 steps a day was linked to 51% lower risk of death than 4,000 steps, whatever the pace.

You don't have to run. You just have to move more.

JAMA Internal Medicine 2015
Using a sauna 4 to 7 times a week was linked to about 50% lower risk of fatal heart disease.

A Finnish study following men for about 20 years. Heat may train your blood vessels a bit like exercise does.

Heart 2016
Loneliness and isolation were linked to 29% higher heart disease and 32% higher stroke risk.

Your relationships are part of your heart health. This is why I look at the whole person.

Your heart responds to the whole of your life. What you eat, how much you move, how you rest and who you spend time with.
Chapter 4

The 5 levers that matter most

01

Cook with olive oil and eat nuts

In PREDIMED, the groups who added extra virgin olive oil or a daily handful of nuts to a Mediterranean diet had about 30% fewer heart attacks, strokes and heart deaths. This is the opposite of the low-fat message, and it's one of the strongest nutrition trials we have.

This week: Make extra virgin olive oil your main cooking oil. Eat a small handful of unsalted nuts most days.
02

Cut the sugar and fried food

Added sugar, fried food and ultra-processed snacks all show up again and again in the heart research. Sugary drinks are the biggest single source of added sugar for most people, so that's the place to start.

This week: Swap sugary drinks for water. Replace one fried or packaged food a day with something you cooked.
03

Build up to 8,000 steps

Going from 4,000 to 8,000 steps a day was linked to about half the risk of death. The pace didn't matter much. It's the total that counts. If you're at 3,000 now, just add 1,000 each week.

This week: Find your current daily step count on your phone. Add 1,000 a day this week.
04

Fix your plate for blood pressure

The DASH trial dropped blood pressure by about 11 points in 8 weeks just by changing food. Fill half your plate with vegetables. Add beans and fruit. Cut back on salty processed food. Ask your doctor whether a potassium salt substitute is right for you.

This week: Fill half your plate with vegetables at lunch and dinner every day.
05

Make time for people

Loneliness was linked to 29% higher heart disease risk. That's in the same range as some of the classic risk factors. Calling a friend, eating with family or joining a walking group is genuinely heart medicine.

This week: Plan one meal or walk with someone you care about this week.

Chapter 6

Mistakes that keep people stuck

  • Buying low-fat processed food. It's often loaded with sugar to make up for the taste.
  • Fearing olive oil and nuts. They were the stars of the biggest trial we have.
  • Thinking you need intense workouts. Steps count. Pace barely mattered.
  • Not knowing your blood pressure. You can't fix what you don't measure.
  • Treating stress and loneliness as soft stuff. The research says otherwise.
  • Stopping heart medication because you feel better. Never do this on your own. Talk to your doctor.
Chapter 7

Safety first

Please read this. Never stop or change heart or blood pressure medication on your own. Salt substitutes contain potassium, so check with your doctor first if you have kidney disease or take blood pressure pills that raise potassium. If you have heart disease, ask your doctor before starting saunas or new exercise. Call 911 right away if you have chest pain or pressure, pain spreading to your arm, jaw or back, sudden shortness of breath, or signs of stroke like face drooping, arm weakness or slurred speech. This guide is education, not medical advice.
