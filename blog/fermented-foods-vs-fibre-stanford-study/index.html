Fermented Foods vs Fibre: What a Stanford Study Found About Your Gut

Stanford tested fermented foods against high fibre for 10 weeks. One group's gut diversity rose and 19 inflammation markers fell. Here's what it means for you.

For years the standard gut advice has been “eat more fibre.” So when a team at Stanford put fibre head to head against fermented foods, most people expected fibre to win. It didn’t.

My story

I’m a high-protein eater, so this study changed how I build my plate. Greek yogurt and kefir pull double duty: protein and live cultures. Mark to fill in: add the fermented food you actually eat most, and how you work it into your day.

The problemModern guts have lost their variety

A healthy gut isn’t about one “good” bacteria. It’s about diversity: lots of different species, each doing different jobs. A less diverse gut tends to go with more inflammation and more gut trouble, and gut problems are now remarkably common. A global study of 73,000 adults found about 40% of those surveyed online met the criteria for at least one functional gut disorder.1

The question is how to get that variety back. Your gut changes quickly. Researchers have watched people’s gut bacteria shift within days of a diet change.2 So which change helps most?

The studyThe Stanford trial: fibre vs fermented foods

The studyCell · 2021

Gut-microbiota-targeted diets modulate human immune status

Human study Randomised trial, 36 healthy adults, 17 weeks in total (10-week diet phase)

What they found. Adults who added fermented foods such as yogurt, kefir, kimchi and kombucha steadily grew more diverse gut bacteria, and 19 inflammation proteins in their blood fell, including interleukin-6. The high-fibre group's diversity stayed flat on average and those 19 markers did not fall. 3

Here’s what struck me. The researchers expected fibre to be the star. Instead the fermented-food group saw their gut diversity rise steadily, and it was the only group whose inflammation markers fell.

The limits. Small trial in healthy adults at Stanford; the fibre group's response depended on the bacteria they started with, so fibre still matters.

How the study worked

Thirty-six healthy adults were randomly split into two groups for ten weeks of a new diet, with time before and after to track the changes.3

What they added Extra fibre from plant foods Yogurt, kefir, fermented cottage cheese, kimchi, fermented vegetables, brine drinks, kombucha
Gut diversity Stayed about the same on average Rose steadily
19 inflammation proteins (incl. IL-6) Did not fall Fell

The fermented-food group also had four types of immune cells showing less activation. Interleukin-6, one of the proteins that fell, is linked to chronic stress, type 2 diabetes and rheumatoid arthritis. And the more fermented food people ate, the stronger the effect.

Why didn’t fibre win?

Fibre isn’t useless. It’s what your gut bacteria eat. The researchers found people on high fibre had more undigested carbohydrate left in their stool, which suggests their gut bacteria couldn’t fully break it down.3 And people’s immune response to fibre depended on how diverse their gut bacteria were to begin with.

My take: if your gut has lost a lot of variety, fibre alone may be like setting a feast for guests who never arrive. Fermented foods bring new guests to the table. You want both.

Do the bacteria in food actually reach your gut?

Some of them do, at least for a while. In the Harvard diet study, researchers found that bacteria, fungi and even viruses from the food people ate turned up living in their gut, settling in temporarily.2 That’s one likely reason fermented foods work best as a daily habit rather than a one-off: you’re topping up visitors that come and go.

It also explains why the Stanford group’s diversity kept climbing through the diet phase instead of jumping once. Every day of fermented food added to the effect.3

What this study doesn’t tell us

I always want you to know the limits. This was 36 healthy adults in California, so we don’t know for sure how it plays out in people with IBS or other gut conditions. It also compared two specific diets, so it can’t tell us whether one particular food (say, kefir) is better than another. And inflammation markers in the blood are an early signal, not the same as preventing disease.3

What it does give us is a clear, testable direction from a well-run randomised trial, and one that’s cheap, safe for most people and easy to try.

The solutionHow to add fermented foods the right way

Choose foods with live cultures

Not every jar on the shelf is alive. Look for these words on the label: live and active cultures, raw, unpasteurised. Most live products sit in the fridge section.

  • Greek yogurt, skyr and kefir (plain, unsweetened): live cultures plus a real dose of protein.
  • Kimchi and sauerkraut (refrigerated, unpasteurised): add a forkful to eggs, meat or salad.
  • Fermented vegetables and brine: pickles made by fermentation, not vinegar.
  • Kombucha: pick low-sugar versions; some are basically soft drinks.

Start small, then build

Go from none to a few spoonfuls a day, then build up over two to three weeks. In the Stanford study, people who ate larger amounts saw stronger effects, but you get there by building, not by starting big. Jumping in too fast is the most common reason people quit because of bloating.

Keep the fibre, pair it with protein

Make protein the anchor of each meal, fill half the plate with vegetables, and put a fermented food on the side. That way your new bacteria have something to eat, and you’re not giving up the steady energy that protein gives you.

By the end of the week you’re having fermented food at more than one meal, without having shocked your gut on day one. Keep building from there if you feel good.

Mistakes that hold people back

  • Buying the sweetened versions. Flavoured yogurts and some kombuchas carry a lot of added sugar. Go plain and add your own fruit.
  • Mistaking vinegar pickles for fermented ones. Shelf-stable, vinegar-based pickles are tasty but usually not alive.
  • Going too big, too fast. A sudden jump can cause gas and bloating. Build up over a few weeks.
  • Expecting food to fix stress. Stress alone can make the gut lining leakier, even in healthy people.4 Fermented foods help most as part of a whole plan that also covers stress, sleep and movement.

Before you start

If you’re immunocompromised, pregnant, or you react badly to fermented foods every time (flushing, headaches, hives), talk to your doctor before adding them. Some people are sensitive to the histamine in fermented foods.

The bottom lineThe bottom line

In the best head-to-head trial we have, fermented foods raised gut diversity and lowered 19 inflammation markers, and a high-fibre diet didn’t on average.3 It was a small trial in healthy adults, so it’s a strong signal rather than the final word. The practical plan is simple: keep your plants, anchor meals with protein, and add a little fermented food every day.

Common questions

Are fermented foods better than fibre for gut health?

In the Stanford trial, fermented foods raised gut diversity and lowered inflammation markers, while extra fibre didn’t on average. But the fibre group’s response depended on the bacteria people started with, so the best plan is both: fermented foods for diversity, and plants for the bacteria to feed on.

Which fermented foods did the Stanford study use?

Yogurt, kefir, fermented cottage cheese, kimchi and other fermented vegetables, vegetable brine drinks and kombucha. People who ate larger amounts saw stronger effects.

Are store-bought pickles and sauerkraut fermented?

Only some. Shelf-stable pickles made with vinegar and heat-treated sauerkraut usually don’t contain live cultures. Look in the fridge section for products labelled raw, unpasteurised or ‘live and active cultures’.

Can fermented foods cause bloating?

They can at first, especially if you go from none to a lot. Start with a few spoonfuls a day and build up over a couple of weeks. If you react badly to fermented foods every time, talk to your doctor; some people are sensitive to histamine.
